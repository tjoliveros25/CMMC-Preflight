The problem

Companies that handle sensitive defense information have to protect it to a federal standard. Proving it costs more than many small shops make on the contract.

$593,800 SBA's estimate of total compliance cost per certification for a small firm
120,000+ Small businesses in the defense industrial base facing the requirement
~100 Approved assessors available to serve all of them

The Department of War suspended the third-party certification phase in July 2026, citing exactly this cost. The requirements themselves did not go away: DFARS 252.204-7012 still applies, self-assessments are still required, and primes still push the obligations down to their subcontractors. A separate DoD cost model puts the assessment alone at $101,752 for a small entity, while a PreVeil survey found 3 in 5 contractors had budgeted under $50,000. How it works

  1. 1

    Export your plan

    Two commands in your Terraform folder produce a plan.json describing your AWS setup.

    terraform plan -out=tfplan
terraform show -json tfplan > plan.json
  2. 2

    Drop it in

    The file is read by code running in your own browser. It is never uploaded, there is no server to send it to, and the site is served with a header that forbids the page from making outbound connections. Open your browser's network tab and watch.

  3. 3

    Read the report

    Every finding names the resource, says why it matters in one sentence, cites the NIST requirement, and gives you Terraform you can paste. Print it or save it as a PDF for your file.

What it is, and what it is not

What it does

  • Reads a real Terraform plan, including resources inside modules
  • Checks 10 technical settings and maps each to a NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 requirement
  • Explains each finding in plain English, with a fix you can paste
  • Says "could not determine" rather than guessing when the plan does not settle a value
  • Runs entirely in your browser, so the file never leaves your machine

What it does not do

  • It is not a certification and does not replace a real assessment
  • It covers 10 of 110 requirements, all technical. Policy, training and physical security are not here
  • It reads what the plan says will be built, not what is running in your account today
  • It produces no SPRS score, and a pass does not mean a requirement is met
  • It needs Terraform, which the smallest shops may not use

Free scanners for Terraform already exist and carry hundreds of rules. They are written for engineers. ReadyCheck is written for the person who has to fill in the self-assessment.

    Questions

    Only 10 of 110 requirements?

    Yes. We built the part a scanner can automate: technical configuration. The other 100 requirements cover policy, training, personnel screening, physical security and incident response, which no tool can read out of a cloud configuration file. Ten checks you can trust is more useful than a hundred you cannot.

    Why not use Checkov, Terrascan or KICS?

    Use them, they are good. They carry hundreds of rules and they are written for engineers, producing rule IDs and severity levels. ReadyCheck answers a different question: what do I write in the self-assessment, and which requirement does this affect? That is why every finding here is one plain sentence with a requirement number and a fix.

    Would I really upload a file describing my network?

    You do not upload it. The checks run as JavaScript in your own browser, there is no server component, and the site is served with a Content Security Policy that forbids the page from opening any outbound connection. Open your browser's network tab while you load a plan and you will see no requests. The code is open, so you can confirm it rather than take our word.

    CMMC is paused. Does any of this still matter?

    Only third-party certification is paused. The 110 requirements of NIST SP 800-171 still apply to anyone handling controlled unclassified information under DFARS 252.204-7012, Phase 1 self-assessments and SPRS scores are still required, and prime contractors still flow the obligations down to their subcontractors.

    What does "could not determine" mean?

    Terraform marks some values as decided only when the plan is applied, and some settings depend on account-wide defaults a plan does not show. Rather than guess, we say so. A wrong pass is the worst thing a readiness tool can give you.

    Does a pass mean I meet the requirement?

    No. It means this one setting, in this plan, looks right. An assessor will want to see the policy behind it, evidence that it is enforced, and that it covers every system in scope, not just the ones Terraform builds. Each finding lists what it does not prove for that reason.

    AWS only?

    For now. The checks read AWS resource types. Azure would be a second set of check files against the same parser and the same report, which is the next thing we would build.
